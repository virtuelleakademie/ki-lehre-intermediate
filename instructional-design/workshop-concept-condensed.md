# Instructional Design in the Age of AI

Working notes on the workshop thesis and design framework.

## Thesis

The central claim of this workshop is that AI has not changed what good teaching requires; it has changed the consequences of getting it wrong. Assessments with structural weaknesses functioned well enough for decades, largely because outsourcing the cognitive work was itself effortful. AI has removed that friction. The design flaws were always present; they simply were not consequential until now. In my view, the productive response is not surveillance, policy, or detection. It is instructional design.


## The Two Questions (Same Question)

Two questions recur in conversations with instructors: "How can I be sure students are doing the cognitive work?" and "My students just won't put in the effort." Both locate the problem in the student (i.e., in honesty or character). However, the evidence consistently points elsewhere: the problem is in the task environment. This distinction matters because it determines where one intervenes. If the problem is the student, the response is monitoring and enforcement. If the problem is the environment, the response is design.


## Diagnosis

**The proxy problem.** Most assessments measure products (i.e., written texts, reports, problem sets) that historically correlated with learning because producing them without understanding was effortful. AI breaks this correlation. The product can now be generated without the cognitive process it was meant to provide evidence for; the proxy no longer works. Crucially, adopting a modern format (e.g., PBL, projects, authentic tasks) does not fix this. If the cognitive task attached to the format is generic (i.e., analyse, summarise, recommend), the format is irrelevant. The underlying problem is not the instructional format; it is the nature of the cognitive demand.

**Policing is counterproductive.** Surveillance reframes the student-instructor relationship as adversarial. Once the implicit goal shifts from "learn the material" to "satisfy the monitoring system," students optimise for the new goal, and that goal invites shortcuts. This is not speculation; it is a prediction from self-determination theory, well-replicated across educational contexts. When perceived autonomy decreases through external controls, intrinsic motivation decreases with it, and strategic circumvention increases. The result is more gaming, not less. Furthermore, detection tools trigger an arms race the instructor cannot win, and the surveillance burden falls disproportionately on students who are already marginalised (e.g., non-native speakers flagged by AI detectors, students with disabilities penalised by proctoring software, first-generation students who lack the cultural fluency to navigate suspicion).

**"Lazy" is the wrong frame.** What looks like laziness is, I would argue, typically something else entirely. In many cases it is rational effort allocation in Simon's sense: if the product is what gets graded, minimising process effort is strategic, not lazy. In other cases it is cognitive overload masquerading as disengagement; working memory is exceeded, and the student is triaging, not shirking. It may also be metacognitive blindness, where students confuse fluency with durability and genuinely believe that reading an AI summary constitutes understanding. This calls for an informational intervention, not a motivational one. Furthermore, the conditions identified by self-determination theory (i.e., competence, autonomy, relatedness) may simply be unmet; these are features of the environment, not the student. Finally, structural realities such as employment, caregiving, financial stress, and operating in a non-native language are often at play. Character attribution forecloses inquiry into all of these.

**The reframe.** The question is not "how do I make sure students do the work?" but rather "how do I design the task environment so that the rational path goes through the cognitive work?" This is Simon's ant parable applied to instruction: the complexity is in the beach, not the ant.


## Core Principle

In my view, the core principle can be stated simply. Good instructional design closes the gap between the cognitive work an instructor intends and the cognitive work the assessment actually requires. When this gap is small, outsourcing is self-defeating; when it is large, outsourcing is rational. Biggs called this constructive alignment. The contribution here is a new diagnostic context: AI as stress test for the alignment between intended and actual cognitive demands.

**Why Bloom's taxonomy is not sufficient.** The revised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) classifies cognitive processes independently of the learner's state. However, element interactivity is determined by learner expertise, not taxonomic category. "Apply procedural knowledge" can be trivial or impossible depending on schema development; the taxonomy provides no mechanism for this variation. Accordingly, alignment between labels (i.e., outcome verbs, activity types, assessment formats) can still produce entirely outsourceable courses. The question is not "what cognitive level?" but "whose cognition?"

**The sharper formulation.** I would claim that the more useful design principle is this: design tasks where the required input is something only the student has access to. AI cannot provide prior mental models, specific confusion points, actual professional experiences, real-time responses to unanticipated questions, or the relationship between what the student thought before and after engaging with the material. These are internal resources that no external tool can substitute for.

**The outsourcing test.** Could someone complete this deliverable without performing the cognitive work I intend? If yes, there is a proxy problem. It is not an AI problem; it is a design problem that AI has made urgent.

**Three categories, not one.** It is worth distinguishing three categories of response: (1) process constraints, where task architecture makes skipping the work structurally pointless; (2) controlled environments, where tool access is restricted because the learning goal is skill automation or learners lack the metacognitive resources to benefit from open access; and (3) surveillance, where the aim is reactive monitoring to detect unauthorised use. The argument against policing targets surveillance, not controlled environments. Controlled environments are legitimate design tools, especially for foundational skill automation and when learners cannot yet evaluate the benefit of effortful practice. The trajectory across a curriculum runs from environmental control to structural design.


## Step Zero: Prior Knowledge

Everything I have outlined above presupposes that the instructor knows what cognitive resources students bring. Without this knowledge, tasks are set at the wrong level and behaviour is misread as laziness. Format choice (e.g., PBL, flipped, project-based) is secondary to schema-task fit. Minimally guided formats overwhelm working memory when schemas are undeveloped (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).

It is important to note that misconceptions are not gaps. They are active, internally coherent schemas that generate systematic errors. They are invisible in product-based assessment; only assessments that externalise reasoning surface them. This point has practical consequences: an instructor who assesses only products will never see the misconceptions that are driving student errors, and will therefore never know where the actual learning problem lies.


## Five Design Principles

None of these principles mention AI. All of them produce AI-resilient assessments. (1) Assess process, not only product; intermediate artefacts make the cognitive trajectory visible. (2) Require situated knowledge; the more a task depends on knowledge from genuine engagement (e.g., a site visit, a discussion, a specific dataset), the less completable at arm's length. (3) Include an oral component; this is arguably the single most powerful and most underused move available to instructors. (4) Make assessment constitutive of learning, such that the assessment activity is the learning activity (i.e., retrieval practice, peer teaching, explanation tasks). (5) Increase specificity and reduce template-ability; genre-shaped tasks (e.g., "write an analysis") can be completed from the template alone, without genuine engagement with the content.


## Four Patterns

All four patterns work across modalities (i.e., online, hybrid, in-person), all welcome AI use, and all make deep processing the efficient path.

**Pattern 1: Prediction and Revision.** Students externalise their prior understanding before instruction, then document what changed. The prediction is submitted before the relevant knowledge is available; the revision is only meaningful relative to a genuine prediction. This pattern is particularly well-suited to direct instruction and lecture formats.

**Pattern 2: Sequential Information Release.** Information is released in stages, and each response builds on the previous one. The cumulative reasoning chain is only coherent if earlier decisions rested on genuine understanding. This pattern works well for PBL, CBL, and design courses.

**Pattern 3: Experience-Anchored Analysis.** Students connect course concepts to actual professional experiences. Real experiences contain odd details and theory-practice friction; fabricated examples tend to be too smooth. This pattern is best suited to professional programmes with concurrent placements.

**Pattern 4: Synchronous Peer Explanation.** Students explain their reasoning to a partner in real time (including via video call). The partner asks follow-up questions; both document what changed. This pattern scales without instructor presence and is particularly useful for online courses and exercises.

The overarching structure across all four patterns is this: each requires an input that exists only inside the student's head, and assesses the connection between this internal input and the external course material.


## Robustness

The claim that AI "cannot satisfy" these criteria is too strong. A sufficiently motivated student can game any pattern. I would argue that the defensible claim is economic, not logical: these patterns change the cost structure so that genuine engagement becomes the path of least resistance. To fabricate a convincing prediction-revision pair, for instance, one must understand the material well enough to identify the conceptual gap; the fabrication requires the understanding it tries to avoid. The argument is analogous to physical locks: no lock is unpickable, but a good lock makes picking harder than using the key.

Three properties are worth noting. First, the cognitive floor is raised even for students who attempt to game the system. Second, these patterns degrade gracefully; partial outsourcing still yields partial learning, unlike traditional assessment where partial outsourcing yields essentially nothing. Third, the comparison class is traditional assignments (i.e., trivially outsourceable), not perfect compliance, which has never existed.


## The Evaluation Paradox

To evaluate whether a shortcut helps one learn, one must already have the knowledge that learning was supposed to produce. Novices cannot judge AI output precisely because judging it requires the expertise the assignment was meant to develop. This is not an argument against AI; it is an argument for designing tasks so that the cognitive work cannot be delegated without consequence.


## What This Workshop Is Not

This workshop does not teach AI detection, AI policy writing, or prompt engineering, and it does not take a position on whether students should use AI. It teaches instructional design. AI serves as a diagnostic lens, not as the subject.
