# Instructional Design in the Age of AI

Notes on the workshop thesis and design framework.

## Thesis

AI has not changed what good teaching requires. It has changed the consequences of getting it wrong. Assessments with structural weaknesses functioned well enough when outsourcing the work was difficult. AI removed that friction. The design flaws were always there; now they are consequential.

The productive response is not surveillance, policy, or detection. It is instructional design.


## The Two Questions (Same Question)

"How can I be sure students are doing the cognitive work?" and "My students just won't put in the effort" locate the problem in the student (honesty or character). The evidence says the problem is in the task environment.


## Diagnosis

**The proxy problem.** Most assessments measure products that historically correlated with learning because producing them without understanding was effortful. AI breaks this correlation. The product can now be generated without the cognitive process it was meant to evidence. The proxy no longer works.

Adopting a modern format (PBL, projects, authentic tasks) does not fix this. If the cognitive task attached to the format is generic (analyze, summarize, recommend), the format is irrelevant.

**Policing is counterproductive.** SDT predicts that increased external control decreases autonomy, which decreases intrinsic motivation, which increases strategic circumvention. Arms race the instructor cannot win. Disproportionate burden on marginalized students.

**"Lazy" is the wrong frame.** What looks like laziness is typically:

- *Rational effort allocation* (Simon). If the product is what gets graded, minimizing process effort is strategic, not lazy.
- *Cognitive overload masquerading as disengagement.* Working memory exceeded. The student is triaging, not shirking.
- *Metacognitive blindness.* Students confuse fluency with durability. They genuinely believe reading an AI summary = understanding. Informational intervention, not motivational.
- *Unmet SDT conditions.* Motivation emerges from competence, autonomy, relatedness. These are features of the environment, not the student.
- *Structural realities.* Employment, caregiving, financial stress, non-native language. Character attribution forecloses inquiry.

**The reframe.** Not "how do I make sure students do the work?" but "how do I design the task environment so the rational path goes through the cognitive work?" Simon's ant parable: the complexity is in the beach, not the ant.


## Core Principle

Good instructional design closes the gap between the cognitive work you intend and the cognitive work your assessment actually requires. Small gap: outsourcing is self-defeating. Large gap: outsourcing is rational.

Biggs called this constructive alignment. The contribution here is a new diagnostic context: AI as stress test.

**Why Bloom's taxonomy is not sufficient.** The revised taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) classifies cognitive processes independently of the learner's state. Element interactivity is determined by learner expertise, not taxonomic category. "Apply procedural knowledge" can be trivial or impossible depending on schema development. The taxonomy provides no mechanism for this variation. Alignment between labels (outcome verbs, activity types, assessment formats) can still produce entirely outsourceable courses.

The question is not "what cognitive level?" but "whose cognition?"

**The sharper formulation.** Design tasks where the required input is something only the student has access to. AI cannot provide: prior mental models, specific confusion points, actual professional experiences, real-time responses to unanticipated questions, the relationship between what the student thought before and after.

**The outsourcing test.** Could someone complete this deliverable without performing the cognitive work I intend? If yes: proxy problem. Not an AI problem. A design problem AI made urgent.

**Three categories, not one.** Process constraints (task architecture makes skipping the work structurally pointless), controlled environments (tool access restricted because the learning goal is skill automation or learners lack metacognitive resources), surveillance (reactive monitoring to detect unauthorized use). The argument against policing targets surveillance, not controlled environments. Controlled environments are legitimate design tools, especially for foundational skill automation and when learners cannot yet evaluate the benefit of effortful practice. The trajectory across a curriculum: from environmental control to structural design.


## Step Zero: Prior Knowledge

Everything presupposes the instructor knows what cognitive resources students bring. Without this: tasks set at the wrong level, behavior misread as laziness.

Format choice (PBL, flipped, project-based) is secondary to schema-task fit. Minimally guided formats overwhelm working memory when schemas are undeveloped (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006).

Misconceptions are not gaps. They are active, internally coherent schemas that generate systematic errors. Invisible in product-based assessment. Only assessments that externalize reasoning surface them.


## Five Design Principles

None mention AI. All produce AI-resilient assessments.

1. **Assess process, not only product.** Intermediate artifacts make the cognitive trajectory visible.
2. **Require situated knowledge.** The more a task depends on knowledge from genuine engagement (site visit, discussion, specific dataset), the less completable at arm's length.
3. **Include an oral component.** Single most powerful, most underused move.
4. **Make assessment constitutive of learning.** The assessment activity *is* the learning activity. Retrieval practice, peer teaching, explanation tasks.
5. **Increase specificity, reduce template-ability.** Genre-shaped tasks ("write an analysis") can be completed from the template alone.


## Four Patterns

All work online, hybrid, in-person. All welcome AI. All make deep processing the efficient path.

**Pattern 1: Prediction and Revision.** Students externalize prior understanding before instruction, then document what changed. Prediction submitted before relevant knowledge available. Revision only meaningful relative to genuine prediction. Best for: direct instruction, lectures.

**Pattern 2: Sequential Information Release.** Information in stages. Each response builds on previous. Cumulative reasoning chain only coherent if earlier decisions rested on genuine understanding. Best for: PBL, CBL, design courses.

**Pattern 3: Experience-Anchored Analysis.** Connect course concepts to actual professional experiences. Real experiences contain odd details, theory-practice friction. Fabricated examples are too smooth. Best for: professional programs with concurrent placements.

**Pattern 4: Synchronous Peer Explanation.** Explain reasoning to partner in real time (including video call). Partner asks follow-ups. Both document what changed. Scales without instructor presence. Best for: online courses, exercises.

**Overarching structure:** All four require an input that exists only inside the student's head and assess the connection between this internal input and external material.


## Robustness

The claim that AI "cannot satisfy" these criteria is too strong. A motivated student can game any pattern. The defensible claim is economic, not logical: **these patterns change the cost structure so genuine engagement becomes the path of least resistance.**

To fabricate a convincing prediction-revision pair, you must understand the material well enough to identify the conceptual gap. The fabrication requires the understanding it tries to avoid. Same argument as physical locks: no lock is unpickable, but a good lock makes picking harder than using the key.

Three properties: (1) cognitive floor is raised even for gaming students, (2) patterns degrade gracefully (partial outsourcing still yields partial learning, unlike traditional assessment where partial outsourcing yields zero), (3) comparison class is traditional assignments (trivially outsourceable), not perfect compliance (never existed).


## The Evaluation Paradox

To evaluate whether a shortcut helps you learn, you must already have the knowledge learning was supposed to produce. Novices cannot judge AI output precisely because judging requires the expertise the assignment was meant to develop. Not an argument against AI. An argument for designing so cognitive work cannot be delegated without consequence.


## What This Workshop Is Not

Does not teach AI detection, AI policy writing, prompt engineering, or take a position on whether students should use AI. It teaches instructional design. AI as diagnostic lens.
