# Ensuring Cognitive Work Without Policing: Assessment Design for the AI Era

Two questions from the workshop, and why they are really the same question.

**Question 1:** "Even if we have determined the cognitive operations that should be performed by the student and not offloaded to AI, how can we be sure students are actually doing them, without having to police them?"

**Question 2:** "My students appear to lack the intrinsic motivation necessary -- otherwise they would do the work. They don't seem to see the necessity of performing the cognitive work, and they seem lazy."


## Part 1: The Diagnosis
 
Both questions locate the problem in the student: their honesty (Question 1) or their character (Question 2). The evidence from cognitive science, instructional design, and educational research converges on a different diagnosis: **the problem is in the task environment, not in the student.**

### Why "policing" doesn't work

Surveillance is not merely impractical; it is counterproductive. When external controls increase (AI detection tools, proctoring software, mandatory monitoring), perceived autonomy decreases, and with it intrinsic motivation. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan) predicts this clearly: controlling environments produce more strategic circumvention, not more genuine engagement. You get the opposite of what you want.

Policing also initiates an arms race that instructors cannot win. It consumes institutional resources that would be better spent on design. And the surveillance burden falls unevenly: students from marginalized backgrounds, students with disabilities, and students for whom the institutional culture is already alienating bear disproportionate costs from policing approaches.

### Why "lazy" is the wrong frame

"Laziness" is not a construct in cognitive science. What we observe as laziness typically reflects one or more of the following, each calling for a different response:

**Rational effort allocation.** Students are boundedly rational agents who satisfice. If a student can get an acceptable outcome without performing the cognitive operations, the task does not sufficiently reward those operations. The student's behavior reveals that the assessment evaluates the *product* (essay, analysis, solution) rather than the *process* (retrieval, generation, integration, monitoring). Minimizing process effort when the product is what gets graded is not lazy. It is strategic. Herbert Simon's concept of bounded rationality applies: people allocate effort where the expected return is highest.

**Cognitive overload masquerading as disengagement.** When total cognitive load exceeds working memory capacity, students disengage. From the outside this looks like laziness. From the inside it feels like drowning. A statistics assignment that simultaneously requires learning new software, understanding a new concept, interpreting output, and writing up results may exceed capacity. The student who uses AI for the write-up is triaging, not being lazy. The solution is to scaffold and sequence: reduce extraneous load so cognitive resources can go toward the operations you want to protect.

**Metacognitive blindness.** Many students genuinely do not understand that effortful processing leads to better learning. They confuse fluency (ease of processing) with durability (long-term retention). A student who reads an AI-generated summary and thinks "I understand this" may honestly believe they have learned the material. They are not dishonest; they lack the metacognitive framework to distinguish recognition from recall. Research by Kornell and Bjork shows that students who are taught about the testing effect subsequently choose to test themselves more often, even when it feels harder. The intervention is informational, not motivational: you change behavior by changing understanding.

**Unmet conditions for motivation.** Intrinsic motivation is not a personality trait that students either have or lack. It emerges from conditions: competence (I can do this), autonomy (I have some choice in how), and relatedness (this matters in a context I care about). When these conditions are absent, so is motivation. That is a design outcome, not a student deficit.

**Structural realities.** When instructors say "lazy," they are often describing behaviors without examining structural conditions. Many students work significant hours alongside their studies. Some are first-generation university students. Some navigate higher education in a non-native language. Some face financial stress, caregiving responsibilities, or mental health challenges. "Lazy" is a character attribution that forecloses inquiry into these realities.

### The reframe

Instead of "my students are lazy," consider: "my students are behaving rationally given the information, incentives, and task designs they encounter."

Instead of "how do I make sure students do the work?", consider: "how do I design the task environment so the rational path goes through the cognitive work?"

This is Simon's ant parable applied to pedagogy. When you watch the ant trace a complex path across the sand, the complexity is not in the ant. The ant follows simple rules. The complexity is in the beach -- the terrain, the obstacles, the contours. Policing tries to change the ant. Good design reshapes the beach.


## Part 2: The General Principle

The usual response to the policing problem is controlled environments: in-class exams, handwritten work, no tools allowed. This works, but only by preventing access. The cognitive work is not done because the task requires it, but because AI is physically unavailable. That is still policing, even if it doesn't feel like it. And it doesn't transfer to online, hybrid, or asynchronous settings.

The sharper design principle: **design tasks where the required input is something only the student has access to.**

AI can generate excellent generic content. What it cannot provide:

- The student's own mental model *before* encountering new information
- The student's specific confusion points and learning trajectory
- Connections to the student's actual professional experiences
- Real-time responses to unanticipated follow-up questions
- The *relationship* between what the student thought before and what they think after

The theoretical foundation: the generation effect works because it activates and reorganizes internal knowledge. Self-explanation works because it processes discrepancies between the student's own model and new input. Both mechanisms require a genuine prior model to exist. Tasks that demand this model as input make the cognitive work the most efficient path to a good grade -- not because AI is banned, but because AI has no access to the required input.

**The goal is to make the strategic path and the learning path identical.** A student who wants the best grade must activate genuine prior knowledge (retrieval), contribute their own predictions or experiences (generation), process discrepancies (linking), and identify their own errors (monitoring). These are the same four operations that build schemas. AI is welcome throughout, but it cannot satisfy the assessment criteria because those criteria rest on inputs that exist only inside the student's head.

### The key distinction: process constraints vs. process surveillance

A process *constraint* is a feature of the task architecture that makes it structurally impossible or pointless to skip the cognitive work. A process *surveillance* mechanism is an attempt to observe whether the work was done. The first changes the beach; the second tries to read the ant's mind. Design for constraints, not surveillance.


## Part 3: Four Design Patterns

Each pattern works in online, hybrid, and in-person settings. Each makes AI welcome. Each makes deep processing the efficient path.

### Pattern 1: Prediction and Revision

**Principle:** Students externalize their prior understanding *before* they encounter new material. After instruction, they document what changed and why.

**Why it works without policing:** The prediction is submitted before the relevant knowledge is available. AI cannot help because the purpose is not correctness but the externalization of a genuine prior belief. The revision is only meaningful relative to a real prediction. A student who fabricates a prediction produces a generic revision. A student with a genuine prediction produces a specific one that references concrete prior misconceptions.

**What to assess:** The quality and specificity of the revision, not the correctness of the prediction. A prediction that turns out to be wrong is often more valuable than one that happens to be right, because the revision reveals deeper processing.

**Best suited for:** Direct instruction, lectures, structured teaching. This pattern wraps around the instruction without changing the instructional format. The lecturer still delivers a structured lecture. But students have a cognitive task *around* the lecture that activates retrieval (prediction), linking (comparison with new material), and monitoring (where was my model wrong?).

#### Example: Statistics lecture (direct instruction, 2nd semester)

*Before the lecture on confidence intervals:* Students submit online (timestamped, completion credit only): "You calculate a mean from a sample of 30 people. How confident are you that the true population value is close? What would you need in order to quantify that confidence?"

*After the lecture:* "How did the lecture change your understanding of 'quantifying confidence'? Where was your prior thinking off, and why?"

A student with a genuine prediction might write: "I thought sample size didn't matter because the mean is the same regardless. Now I understand that the variability of sample means depends on sample size, and that's exactly what the confidence interval quantifies." A student who fabricated a prediction writes: "I learned that confidence intervals are useful for quantifying uncertainty." The difference in specificity is diagnostic without requiring any surveillance.

#### Example: Lecture on infectious diseases (health sciences)

The instructor shows a clinical image with symptom description. Before the explanation, students type into an online form: "What is your suspected diagnosis, and which two findings support it?" After the explanation: "How did your diagnosis change? Which finding did you over- or underweight, and why?"

#### Example: Structural engineering lecture (architecture)

Before explaining a new structural principle: "Sketch how you would transfer the load in this building, and justify your choice." After the lecture: "Compare your sketch to the principle presented. Where was your approach correct, where wrong, and what was the crucial error in your reasoning?"


### Pattern 2: Sequential Information Release

**Principle:** Information is released in stages. Students respond to each stage before the next becomes available. Each response must reference and build on the previous one.

**Why it works without policing:** AI can help at every stage. But the cumulative reasoning chain -- how new information revises earlier decisions -- is only coherent if the earlier decisions rested on genuine understanding. A student who submitted an AI-generated response at Stage 1 cannot convincingly explain in Stage 2 *why* certain decisions were made and how new constraints affect those decisions. The specificity of revisions across stages is the diagnostic marker.

**What to assess:** The coherence and specificity of the reasoning chain across stages. Does each stage genuinely build on the previous one? Can the student articulate *why* a new piece of information changes their earlier reasoning?

**Best suited for:** Problem-based learning, challenge-based learning, case-based instruction, design courses. Any context where a complex problem unfolds over time.

#### Example: Civil engineering -- pedestrian bridge design (challenge-based learning)

A municipal council commissions a planning team to design a pedestrian bridge over a river. The challenge unfolds in four stages:

*Stage 1 (Week 1):* Site photos, river data (width, flood markers), budget. Task: "Sketch two fundamentally different structural concepts. Justify your decisions."

*Stage 2 (Week 2):* A geological survey reveals difficult soil conditions on one bank. Task: "How does this finding change your concepts? Which concept is more affected, and why?"

*Stage 3 (Week 3):* New requirement from the municipality: wheelchair accessibility with a maximum gradient of 6%. Task: "Revise your preferred concept. Where do conflicts arise with your earlier decisions?"

*Stage 4 (Week 4):* Cost calculation and comparison with an AI-generated design variant. Task: "Compare your concept with the AI version. Where do the underlying assumptions differ? Which assumptions are better justified?"

A student who deeply engaged in Stage 1 has rich, specific material to revise in Stage 2 ("My cantilever concept assumed solid rock on both banks; the geological report means I need to redesign the eastern foundation entirely, which changes the cost balance between my two concepts"). A student who submitted a generic AI output in Stage 1 produces a generic revision in Stage 2.


### Pattern 3: Experience-Anchored Analysis

**Principle:** Tasks require connecting course concepts to the student's actual professional experiences (from internships, clinical placements, work).

**Why it works without policing:** AI can invent a generic professional scenario. But the assessment criteria reward specificity and idiosyncrasy: real experiences contain odd details, unexpected turns, and moments where theory does not fit neatly. Fabricated examples are narratively too smooth and fit the theory too perfectly. Additionally, if students know they may face a follow-up question (even asynchronously, in a forum or a brief video call), fabrication collapses quickly, because real experiences become richer on follow-up and invented ones become thinner.

**What to assess:** Specificity and depth of the theory-practice connection. Does the student identify where the theory fits well *and* where it falls short? Genuine application always involves friction; frictionless application is a sign of fabrication.

**Best suited for:** Professional programs (nursing, social work, engineering, business), practicum supervision, any course where students have concurrent professional experience.

#### Example: Social work -- counseling skills seminar

"Describe a specific counseling conversation from your practicum where communication became difficult at a particular point. (a) What exactly happened? Describe the critical moment in as much detail as possible. (b) Analyze this moment using the Four-Ears Model: which level was dominant, and what evidence supports your reading? (c) What could you have done differently, and which level would that have addressed?"

#### Example: Nursing -- clinical decision-making

"Describe a situation from your clinical placement where you observed a patient assessment that surprised you -- where the clinical picture did not match your expectation based on textbook presentations. What did you expect, what did you observe, and what does the discrepancy teach you about the limits of textbook knowledge?"


### Pattern 4: Synchronous Peer Explanation

**Principle:** Students explain their reasoning to a partner in real time (including via video call). The partner asks follow-up questions. Both document what they took away from the exchange.

**Why it works without policing:** Real-time explanation requires retrieval and generation in the moment. When a partner asks unexpected follow-up questions, there is no detour through AI. The subsequent reflection ("What was the most important question my partner asked, and how did it change my understanding?") is only meaningful if the conversation was genuine. This pattern scales because the instructor does not need to be present. Students organize the conversations independently.

**What to assess:** The reflection on what changed through the exchange. Genuine reflections reference specific moments in the conversation ("When my partner asked why I allocated the fixed costs that way, I realized I had confused..."). Generic reflections reference nothing specific.

**Best suited for:** Online courses, exercise sessions, tutorials, any format where synchronous pair work is feasible (including via Zoom or similar tools).

#### Example: Business administration -- online cost accounting course

Students individually complete a break-even analysis task. Then pairs are formed (Zoom, 15 minutes). Person A explains to Person B: "How did I arrive at the break-even quantity, and why did I allocate the fixed costs this way?" Person B asks at least two clarifying questions. Then they switch roles. Both submit: "What was the most important question my partner asked, and how did it change my understanding?"

#### Example: Computer science -- algorithm design

Students individually solve an optimization problem. In pairs (15 minutes, video call), each explains their solution approach. The partner's task is to find a case where the approach fails or is suboptimal. Both submit: "What edge case did my partner identify, and how does it change my solution?"


## Part 4: Direct Instruction and the Prediction-Revision Loop

A common concern: "These patterns seem to require open, student-centered formats. I teach structured lectures. Does this apply to me?"

Yes. The prediction-revision loop (Pattern 1) is specifically designed for direct instruction. It wraps *around* the lecture without changing the lecture itself:

1. **Before** instruction: Students externalize their prior understanding (short prediction, concept map, brief answer)
2. **During** instruction: The new material provides the comparison standard
3. **After** instruction: Students document how their model changed

This transforms passive reception into active processing without requiring the instructor to abandon lectures. The students have a cognitive task around the lecture that activates retrieval (prediction), linking (comparison with new material), and monitoring (where was my model wrong?).

The instructor's role does not change. The instruction remains structured and explicit. What changes is that students arrive at the lecture with an activated prior model, which makes the lecture itself more effective because new information has something to connect to. This is consistent with what we know about how prior knowledge interacts with instruction: activated prior knowledge creates anchor points for new material, and discrepancies between prior beliefs and new information drive deeper processing.


## Part 5: The Overarching Pattern

All four patterns share the same underlying structure:

| Pattern | Student-internal input | What AI cannot supply | Assessment focus |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Prediction-Revision | Prior understanding before instruction | The student's actual mental model | Quality and specificity of revision |
| Sequential Release | Decisions under uncertainty | The reasoning behind earlier decisions | Coherence of argument chain across stages |
| Experience-Anchored | Actual professional experience | Specific, idiosyncratic real-world details | Depth of theory-practice connection |
| Peer Explanation | Real-time thinking process | Spontaneous responses to unanticipated questions | Reflection on what changed through exchange |

The task demands an input that exists only inside the student's head, and assesses the quality of the connection between this internal input and the external material. AI has no access to the internal input -- not because it is banned, but because it originates *before* contact with the material or from actual personal experience.

This makes the strategic path and the learning path identical. The student who wants the best grade must activate genuine prior knowledge (retrieval), contribute their own predictions or experiences (generation), process discrepancies (linking), and identify their own errors (monitoring). These are the four cognitive operations that build schemas.


## Part 6: How Robust Are These Patterns, Really?

The claim that AI "cannot satisfy" criteria based on student-internal inputs is too strong. A sufficiently motivated student can game any pattern. Someone could ask AI to generate a plausible misconception and a convincing revision. Someone could fabricate a professional experience with realistic messy details. The strong version of the claim is false, and any skeptical lecturer will see through it immediately.

The defensible claim is economic, not logical: **these patterns change the cost structure so that genuine cognitive engagement becomes the path of least resistance.**

Consider what it takes to fabricate a convincing prediction-revision pair. The student must generate a plausible wrong prediction (not any wrong answer, but one a real student would hold), understand the correct answer well enough to identify the precise conceptual gap, and articulate the transition. That second step is the trap: to fabricate the revision convincingly, you must understand the material. The fabrication requires the understanding it is trying to avoid. At some point, fabrication costs more effort than genuine engagement, and a boundedly rational agent who satisfices will take the genuine path.

This is the same argument that applies to physical locks. No lock is unpickable. But a good lock makes picking harder than using the key. You do not need a perfect lock. You need one where the legitimate path is easier than the illegitimate one.

Three properties make this argument hold:

**The cognitive floor is raised.** Even a student who games every pattern still does more cognitive work than one who pastes an assignment into ChatGPT and submits the output. At minimum, they must retrieve genuine prior beliefs, identify real experiences, or be present in real conversations. The floor of engagement is higher even for the most strategic student.

**The patterns degrade gracefully.** A student who submits a genuine prediction but outsources the revision to AI has still done retrieval (recalling what they believed) and some comparison (recognizing the lecture said something different). They have outsourced the deepest operation (articulating *why* the prediction was wrong), and they learn less than someone who processes the discrepancy themselves. But they learn more than someone who never made a prediction at all. Under traditional product-only assessment, partial outsourcing yields zero learning. Under these patterns, partial outsourcing still yields partial learning.

**The comparison class matters.** These patterns are not competing against perfect compliance (which never existed). They are competing against traditional assignments, which are trivially outsourced to AI with near-zero cognitive engagement. Against that baseline, every pattern described here is a substantial improvement.

The honest position for workshop participants: these patterns do not guarantee that every student will do every cognitive operation. No assessment design ever has, and no assessment design ever will. What they do is make genuine engagement the cheaper, easier, more natural path. For a population of boundedly rational satisficers, that is the strongest achievable guarantee.


## Part 7: What to Tell Lecturers Who Say "My Students Are Lazy"

The reframe, in practical terms:

1. **Check your task design first.** If students can complete the assignment by outsourcing the cognitive operations and still produce an acceptable output, the problem is that your task architecture has made the cognitive work separable from the product. Redesign the task so the operations are inherent, not incidental.

2. **Check the difficulty level.** If tasks are too far beyond students' current schema development, they experience unproductive struggle, not desirable difficulty. What looks like laziness may be rational avoidance of cognitive overload. Scaffold and sequence: use worked examples for beginners, completion problems for intermediates, full problems only for those with sufficient schemas.

3. **Teach students why effort matters.** Many students genuinely believe that reading an AI summary is equivalent to working through a problem themselves. They are wrong, but they are not dishonest. Explain the distinction between fluency and learning explicitly: "If this feels hard, that is by design. The difficulty is the point. If it felt easy, you should be concerned." Name the concept of desirable difficulties. Make metacognitive awareness part of the curriculum.

4. **Align incentives.** If you reward products, you will get products (potentially AI-generated). If you reward reasoning processes, you will get reasoning. Make the assessment system reward exactly the cognitive operations you value. Use the four patterns above to make the operations visible in the output.

5. **Provide credible rationales.** Not "because learning science says so," but "because when you are in professional practice and your AI tool gives you a plausible-sounding but subtly wrong recommendation, you will need the ability to detect that error. That ability comes from having done the integration work yourself, repeatedly."

6. **Accept the limits of your influence honestly.** You cannot make every student deeply engage with every cognitive operation in every assignment. This was true before AI. What you can do is create conditions where the rational path goes through the cognitive work. Beyond that, students are adults making choices in complex life circumstances. Design for the students you have, not the students you wish you had.

The arrival of AI does not change the fundamental principle. It merely makes the consequences of poor task design more visible, because AI provides a frictionless path for cognitive outsourcing that was previously more effortful. The solution was never surveillance. The solution was, and remains, better design.
