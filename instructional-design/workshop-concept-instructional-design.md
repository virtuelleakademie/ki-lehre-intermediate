# Workshop Concept: Instructional Design in the Age of AI

## Thesis

AI has not changed what good teaching requires. What it has changed is that instructors now feel the urgency of getting instructional design right. For decades, assessments with structural weaknesses functioned well enough because outsourcing the work was difficult. AI has removed that friction. The design flaws have always been there; now, they are consequential.

This workshop takes the position that the productive response to AI is not surveillance, policy, or detection. It is instructional design: aligning what we want students to learn with what we ask them to do and how we evaluate whether they did it.

## The Problem: Two Questions

Two questions come up in every workshop on AI in higher education, and they are really the same question.

**Question 1:** "How can I be sure my students are actually doing the cognitive work and not just outsourcing it to AI?"

**Question 2:** "My students just won't put in the effort. They take the path of least resistance every time."

Both questions locate the problem in the student: their honesty (Question 1) or their character (Question 2). The evidence from cognitive science, instructional design, and educational research converges on a different diagnosis: the problem is in the task environment, not in the student.

## The Diagnosis

### The proxy problem

The instinct when students outsource cognitive work to AI is to treat it as a technology problem requiring a technology response: detection tools, usage policies, proctored environments. This framing is understandable but misleading. The problem is older than AI, and the solution is not technological.

Many courses have adopted formats that appear modern: projects, authentic tasks, applied problems, problem-based or inquiry-based approaches. The frustration is genuine. Instructors have invested in pedagogical reform and feel betrayed when students outsource the work anyway. But adopting a modern format does not automatically produce a well-designed assessment. If the cognitive task attached to the format is generic (analyze, summarize, recommend), the format is irrelevant. The task can be completed without engaging the specifics. This was true when students copied from each other or hired ghostwriters. AI made it obvious.

Most assessments do not directly measure learning. They measure products (essays, reports, presentations, solutions) that historically correlated with learning because producing them without understanding was effortful. AI breaks this correlation. The product can now be generated without the cognitive process it was meant to evidence. The proxy no longer works.

### Why policing is counterproductive

Surveillance does not merely fail to solve the problem; it compounds it. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan) predicts that when external controls increase, perceived autonomy decreases, and with it intrinsic motivation. The predicted result is increased strategic circumvention rather than genuine engagement. Policing also initiates an arms race that instructors cannot win, consumes instditutional resources better spent on design, and imposes disproportionate costs on students from marginalized backgrounds, students with disabilities, and those for whom the institutional culture is already alienating.

### Why "lazy" is the wrong frame

Cognitive science does not use "laziness" as an explanatory construct. Breaking the observed behavior into its components reveals more actionable causes:

**Rational effort allocation.** Students are boundedly rational agents who satisfice (Simon). If a student can achieve an acceptable outcome without performing the cognitive operations, the task does not sufficiently reward those operations. The assessment evaluates the *product* rather than the *process*, and minimizing process effort is strategic, not lazy.

**Cognitive overload masquerading as disengagement.** When total cognitive load exceeds working memory capacity, students disengage. An assignment that simultaneously requires learning new software, understanding a new concept, interpreting output, and writing up results may exceed capacity. The student who uses AI for the write-up is triaging. The solution is to reduce extraneous load so cognitive resources go toward the operations that matter.

**Metacognitive blindness.** Many students genuinely do not understand that effortful processing leads to better learning. They confuse fluency (ease of processing) with durability (long-term retention). Research by Kornell and Bjork shows that students taught about the testing effect subsequently choose to test themselves more often, even when it feels harder. The intervention is informational, not motivational.

These cognitive factors are compounded by motivational and structural realities. Intrinsic motivation is not a personality trait; it emerges from conditions (competence, autonomy, relatedness) that are features of the learning environment. And many students navigate significant competing demands: employment, caregiving, financial stress, or study in a non-native language. "Lazy" is a character attribution that forecloses inquiry into both cognitive and structural causes.

### The reframe

Instead of "how do I make sure students do the work?", consider: "how do I design the task environment so the rational path goes through the cognitive work?"

This is Simon's ant parable applied to pedagogy. The complexity of the ant's path across the sand is not in the ant, which follows simple rules. It is in the beach: the terrain, the obstacles, the contours. Good design reshapes the beach.


## The Core Principle

Good instructional design closes the gap between the cognitive work you intend and the cognitive work your assessment actually requires. When this gap is small, outsourcing becomes self-defeating: skipping the work means missing the learning, and the assessment makes this visible. When the gap is large, outsourcing is rational, and nothing in the design catches it.

This principle is not new. Biggs called it constructive alignment. The contribution of this workshop is not a new theory but a new diagnostic context: AI as a stress test for instructional design.

### Why the revised taxonomy is not sufficient

Constructive alignment is typically operationalized through the revised Bloom's taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). The revision made genuine improvements over the 1956 original: a knowledge dimension (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive) drawing on real distinctions from cognitive psychology, more specific subcategories for each cognitive process, and a relaxed hierarchy closer to a continuum with acknowledged overlaps. These are real advances, and the revised taxonomy remains a useful tool for specifying learning outcomes.

Its limitation is specific: the taxonomy classifies cognitive processes independently of the learner's state. The difficulty of any cognitive operation depends on the interaction between task demands and existing schemas. Cognitive load theory calls this element interactivity, and it is determined by learner expertise, not by the taxonomic category of the task. The two-dimensional matrix (cognitive process by knowledge type) captures what kind of knowledge is involved but not how much of it the student already has. A task classified as "apply procedural knowledge" may be trivial for one student and impossible for another, depending entirely on their schema development. The taxonomy provides no mechanism for this variation.

The consequence for assessment design: outcomes that are well-specified in the revised taxonomy's terms can still produce a course where the cognitive work is entirely outsourceable, because the alignment is between labels (outcome verbs, activity types, assessment formats) rather than between cognitive operations and task architecture. This workshop asks a different question. Not "what cognitive level should this task target?" but "which cognitive operations build schemas, and does the task architecture structurally require them?" The four operations (retrieval, generation, linking, monitoring) are grounded in how learning works, not in a classification of intended outcomes. The question is not "what cognitive level?" but "whose cognition?"

### The sharper formulation

**Design tasks where the required input is something only the student has access to.**

AI can generate excellent generic content. What it cannot provide:

- The student's own mental model *before* encountering new information
- The student's specific confusion points and learning trajectory
- Connections to the student's actual professional experiences
- Real-time responses to unanticipated follow-up questions
- The *relationship* between what the student thought before and what they think after

The generation effect works because it activates and reorganizes internal knowledge. Self-explanation works because it processes discrepancies between the student's own model and new input. Both mechanisms require a genuine prior model to exist. Tasks that demand this model as input make the cognitive work the most efficient path to a good grade, not because AI is banned, but because AI has no access to the required input. The strategic path and the learning path become identical.

### The Outsourcing Test

The central diagnostic tool of the workshop is a single question applied to any assessment:

*Could someone complete this deliverable without performing the cognitive work I intend?*

If the answer is yes, the assessment has a proxy problem. This is not an AI problem. It is a design problem that AI has made urgent. The test applies equally to outsourcing via AI, via peers, or via contract cheating.

### Process constraints, controlled environments, and surveillance

These are three distinct categories, and the document's argument applies differently to each.

A *process constraint* is a feature of the task architecture that makes it structurally impossible or pointless to skip the cognitive work. The task itself requires inputs only the student has. This is the primary strategy advocated here.

A *controlled environment* restricts tool access to create conditions where specific cognitive operations must occur. A calculator-free arithmetic exam, a closed-book test designed for retrieval practice, a clinical skills assessment where the student must perform the procedure manually: these are not failures of task design that surveillance compensates for. They are task design. The instructor has identified a cognitive operation that needs to happen, determined that a tool would bypass it, and structured the environment accordingly.

Controlled environments are a legitimate and sometimes necessary design tool, particularly in two circumstances. First, when the learning goal is skill automation: building the automated procedures (mental computation, pattern recognition, manual technique) that form the foundation for later expertise. A tool can perform the operation, but the learner needs to be able to perform it too, eventually without conscious effort. The whole point of practice is to build that automaticity, and no task redesign changes the fact that the tool is faster. Second, when learners lack the metacognitive resources to choose effortful practice independently. The evaluation paradox applies reflexively: appreciating why foundational practice matters requires the very expertise that practice is meant to develop. A primary school child cannot evaluate the long-term benefit of arithmetic fluency. A novice in any domain may not be able to. Below a certain metacognitive threshold, expecting learners to choose the harder path is an appeal to individual responsibility, not a design solution.

*Surveillance*, by contrast, is reactive monitoring that attempts to detect unauthorized tool use after the fact: AI detection software, plagiarism checkers used as gatekeepers, proctoring tools that flag suspicious behavior. These do not create conditions for learning; they try to catch the absence of it. It is this category, not controlled environments, that the argument against policing targets.

The practical implication: as learners develop expertise and metacognitive capacity, the balance should shift from controlled environments toward process constraints. Foundational courses may appropriately restrict tool access. Advanced courses should increasingly rely on task architectures where the cognitive work is the efficient path. The trajectory across a curriculum is from environmental control to structural design.


## Step Zero: Assessing Prior Knowledge and Misconceptions

Everything in this document presupposes that the instructor knows, at least approximately, what cognitive resources students bring to the task. Without this knowledge, tasks will be set at the wrong level, and the resulting behavior will be misread as laziness or dishonesty when it is actually a predictable consequence of mismatch.

### Format choice is secondary to schema-task fit

Considerable energy in higher education goes into choosing instructional formats: problem-based learning, flipped classrooms, project-based approaches. What determines whether any of them works is the match between the format's cognitive demands and the student's current schema development. Minimally guided formats place high demands on working memory. When students have well-developed schemas, much of this processing is automated and the demands are manageable. When they do not, working memory is overwhelmed and the student experiences unproductive struggle rather than desirable difficulty. Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006) made this argument directly: minimally guided instruction is effective for students with sufficient prior knowledge and counterproductive for those without it.

A course can therefore adopt every pedagogical innovation available and still fail if the scaffolding does not match what students actually know. A student who turns to AI may not be disengaged; the task may simply exceed current capacity.

### Misconceptions are not gaps

Students do not arrive as blank slates. Misconceptions are not merely the absence of correct knowledge; they are active, internally coherent schemas that generate systematic errors. A student who believes that a larger sample "contains more opinions and is therefore less precise" will produce wrong answers consistently and confidently. This student does not need more information; they need their existing model to be surfaced, challenged, and restructured.

Misconceptions are invisible in product-based assessment. A student with a fundamental misconception can still produce plausible output, especially with AI assistance. Only assessments that require students to externalize their reasoning will surface these errors.

### Practical implications

**Assess prior knowledge before designing tasks.** Short diagnostic tasks (predictions, concept maps, "what do you think happens when..." questions) administered at the start of a module reveal where students actually are. The prediction-revision pattern (Pattern 1 below) serves this diagnostic function while simultaneously producing learning.

**Use prior knowledge data to calibrate scaffolding.** If students lack the schemas needed for a minimally guided task, provide more structure: worked examples, completion problems, process worksheets. If they already have strong schemas, reduce scaffolding and increase the generative demand. This should vary across a course as schemas develop.

**Design for misconception surfacing.** Tasks that ask students to predict, explain, or apply before receiving instruction force misconceptions into the open. Tasks that only deliver correct information leave misconceptions intact beneath new vocabulary.

**Expect the expertise reversal effect.** What works for novices (high guidance, structured scaffolding) becomes counterproductive for more advanced students, and vice versa. Instructional design is a matter of matching the method to the learner's current state and adjusting as that state changes.


## Five Design Principles

The workshop organizes its redesign work around five principles. None of them mention AI. All of them produce assessments that happen to be AI-resilient.

**Assess the process, not only the product.** When only the final deliverable is evaluated, the path to that deliverable is invisible. Intermediate artifacts (drafts, decision logs, revision histories, annotated sources) make the cognitive trajectory visible and make it far more difficult to fabricate a plausible trajectory of thought.

**Require situated knowledge.** An assessment is vulnerable when everything needed to complete it is publicly available and context-free. The more a task depends on knowledge acquired through genuine engagement (a site visit, a discussion, a specific dataset), the less it can be completed at arm's length.

**Include an oral component.** A brief oral exchange in which the student explains their reasoning, responds to a follow-up question, or defends a decision collapses the gap between product and understanding almost entirely. This is the single most powerful and most underused move available.

**Make the assessment constitutive of learning.** The strongest assessments are those where the assessment activity *is* the learning activity. Retrieval practice, elaborative interrogation, peer teaching, and explanation tasks all have this property: doing the work produces the learning, and skipping the work forfeits it.

**Increase specificity, reduce template-ability.** The more a task resembles a genre ("write an analysis," "produce a summary," "submit a reflection"), the more it can be completed from the genre template alone, without engaging the specific content.


## Four Design Patterns

Each pattern works in online, hybrid, and in-person settings. Each makes AI welcome. Each makes deep processing the efficient path.

### Pattern 1: Prediction and Revision

**Principle:** Students externalize their prior understanding *before* they encounter new material. After instruction, they document what changed and why.

**Why it works:** The prediction is submitted before the relevant knowledge is available. AI cannot help because the purpose is not correctness but the externalization of a genuine prior belief. The revision is only meaningful relative to a real prediction. A fabricated prediction produces a generic revision; a genuine prediction produces a specific one that references concrete prior misconceptions.

**What to assess:** The quality and specificity of the revision, not the correctness of the prediction. A wrong prediction is often more valuable than a correct one, because the revision reveals deeper processing.

**Best suited for:** Direct instruction, lectures, structured teaching. This pattern wraps around the instruction without changing the instructional format. It transforms passive reception into active processing: students arrive with an activated prior model, which creates anchor points for new material and drives deeper processing through discrepancy detection.

#### Example: Statistics lecture

*Before the lecture on confidence intervals:* Students submit online (timestamped, completion credit only): "You calculate a mean from a sample of 30 people. How confident are you that the true population value is close? What would you need in order to quantify that confidence?"

*After the lecture:* "How did the lecture change your understanding of 'quantifying confidence'? Where was your prior thinking off, and why?"

A student with a genuine prediction might write: "I thought sample size didn't matter because the mean is the same regardless. Now I understand that the variability of sample means depends on sample size, and that's exactly what the confidence interval quantifies." A student who fabricated a prediction writes: "I learned that confidence intervals are useful for quantifying uncertainty." The difference in specificity is diagnostic without requiring any surveillance.


### Pattern 2: Sequential Information Release

**Principle:** Information is released in stages. Students respond to each stage before the next becomes available. Each response must reference and build on the previous one.

**Why it works:** AI can help at every stage. But the cumulative reasoning chain is only coherent if the earlier decisions rested on genuine understanding. A student who submitted an AI-generated response at Stage 1 cannot convincingly explain in Stage 2 *why* certain decisions were made and how new constraints affect those decisions. The specificity of revisions across stages is the diagnostic marker.

**What to assess:** The coherence and specificity of the reasoning chain across stages. Does each stage genuinely build on the previous one?

**Best suited for:** Problem-based learning, challenge-based learning, design courses. Any context where a complex problem unfolds over time.

#### Example: Civil engineering (challenge-based learning)

A planning team designs a pedestrian bridge. Stage 1 provides site data and budget; students sketch two structural concepts. Stage 2 reveals difficult soil conditions on one bank; students revise. Stage 3 adds an accessibility requirement; students identify conflicts with earlier decisions. Stage 4 compares their concept with an AI-generated variant; students evaluate differing assumptions. A student who genuinely engaged in Stage 1 has rich material to revise in Stage 2. A student who submitted generic output in Stage 1 produces a generic revision in Stage 2.


### Pattern 3: Experience-Anchored Analysis

**Principle:** Tasks require connecting course concepts to the student's actual professional experiences (internships, clinical placements, work).

**Why it works:** AI can invent a generic professional scenario. But real experiences contain odd details, unexpected turns, and moments where theory does not fit neatly. Fabricated examples are narratively too smooth and fit the theory too perfectly. If students know they may face a follow-up question, fabrication collapses quickly: real experiences become richer on follow-up and invented ones become thinner.

**What to assess:** Specificity and depth of the theory-practice connection. Does the student identify where theory fits well *and* where it falls short? Genuine application always involves friction; frictionless application is a sign of fabrication.

**Best suited for:** Professional programs (nursing, social work, engineering, business), practicum supervision, any course where students have concurrent professional experience.

#### Example: Clinical decision-making (nursing)

"Describe a situation from your clinical placement where a patient assessment surprised you, where the clinical picture did not match your expectation based on textbook presentations. What did you expect, what did you observe, and what does the discrepancy teach you about the limits of textbook knowledge?"


### Pattern 4: Synchronous Peer Explanation

**Principle:** Students explain their reasoning to a partner in real time (including via video call). The partner asks follow-up questions. Both document what they took away from the exchange.

**Why it works:** Real-time explanation requires retrieval and generation in the moment. When a partner asks unexpected follow-up questions, there is no detour through AI. The subsequent reflection is only meaningful if the conversation was genuine. This pattern scales because the instructor does not need to be present.

**What to assess:** The reflection on what changed through the exchange. Genuine reflections reference specific moments ("When my partner asked why I allocated the fixed costs that way, I realized I had confused..."). Generic reflections reference nothing specific.

**Best suited for:** Online courses, exercise sessions, tutorials, any format where synchronous pair work is feasible.

#### Example: Algorithm design (computer science)

Students individually solve an optimization problem. In pairs (15 minutes, video call), each explains their solution approach. The partner's task is to find a case where the approach fails or is suboptimal. Both submit: "What edge case did my partner identify, and how does it change my solution?"


## The Overarching Pattern

All four patterns share the same underlying structure:

| Pattern | Student-internal input | What AI cannot supply | Assessment focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prediction-Revision | Prior understanding before instruction | The student's actual mental model | Quality and specificity of revision |
| Sequential Release | Decisions under uncertainty | The reasoning behind earlier decisions | Coherence of argument chain across stages |
| Experience-Anchored | Actual professional experience | Specific, idiosyncratic real-world details | Depth of theory-practice connection |
| Peer Explanation | Real-time thinking process | Spontaneous responses to unanticipated questions | Reflection on what changed through exchange |

The task demands an input that exists only inside the student's head and assesses the quality of the connection between this internal input and the external material. AI has no access to the internal input, not because it is banned, but because it originates *before* contact with the material or from actual personal experience.


## How Robust Are These Patterns?

The claim that AI "cannot satisfy" criteria based on student-internal inputs is too strong. A sufficiently motivated student can game any pattern. Someone could ask AI to generate a plausible misconception and a convincing revision. Someone could fabricate a professional experience with realistic messy details. The strong version of the claim is false, and any skeptical lecturer will see through it immediately.

The defensible claim is economic, not logical: **these patterns change the cost structure so that genuine cognitive engagement becomes the path of least resistance.**

Consider what it takes to fabricate a convincing prediction-revision pair. The student must generate a plausible wrong prediction (not any wrong answer, but one a real student would hold), understand the correct answer well enough to identify the precise conceptual gap, and articulate the transition. That second step is the trap: to fabricate the revision convincingly, you must understand the material. The fabrication requires the understanding it is trying to avoid. At some point, fabrication costs more effort than genuine engagement, and a boundedly rational agent who satisfices will take the genuine path.

This is the same argument that applies to physical locks. No lock is unpickable. But a good lock makes picking harder than using the key. You do not need a perfect lock. You need one where the legitimate path is easier than the illegitimate one.

Three properties make this argument hold:

**The cognitive floor is raised.** Even a student who games every pattern still does more cognitive work than one who pastes an assignment into ChatGPT and submits the output. At minimum, they must retrieve genuine prior beliefs, identify real experiences, or be present in real conversations. The floor of engagement is higher even for the most strategic student.

**The patterns degrade gracefully.** A student who submits a genuine prediction but outsources the revision to AI has still done retrieval (recalling what they believed) and some comparison (recognizing the lecture said something different). They have outsourced the deepest operation (articulating *why* the prediction was wrong), and they learn less than someone who processes the discrepancy themselves. But they learn more than someone who never made a prediction at all. Under traditional product-only assessment, partial outsourcing yields zero learning. Under these patterns, partial outsourcing still yields partial learning.

**The comparison class matters.** These patterns are not competing against perfect compliance, which never existed. They are competing against traditional assignments, which are trivially outsourced to AI with near-zero cognitive engagement. Against that baseline, every pattern described here is a substantial improvement.

These patterns do not guarantee that every student will do every cognitive operation. No assessment design ever has, and no assessment design ever will. What they do is make genuine engagement the cheaper, easier, more natural path. For a population of boundedly rational satisficers, that is the strongest achievable guarantee.


## Responding to "My Students Are Lazy"

1. **Check task design.** If students can complete the assignment by outsourcing the cognitive operations and still produce an acceptable output, the task architecture has made the cognitive work separable from the product. Redesign so the operations are inherent, not incidental.

2. **Check the difficulty level.** Tasks too far beyond students' current schema development produce unproductive struggle, not desirable difficulty. Scaffold and sequence: worked examples for beginners, completion problems for intermediates, full problems only for those with sufficient schemas.

3. **Teach students why effort matters.** Many students genuinely believe that reading an AI summary is equivalent to working through a problem themselves. Explain the distinction between fluency and learning explicitly. Name the concept of desirable difficulties. Make metacognitive awareness part of the curriculum.

4. **Align incentives.** If the assessment system rewards products, it will get products. If it rewards reasoning processes, it will get reasoning.

5. **Provide credible rationales.** Not "because learning science says so," but "because in professional practice, when your AI tool gives you a plausible-sounding but subtly wrong recommendation, you will need the ability to detect that error. That ability comes from having done the integration work yourself, repeatedly."

6. **Accept the limits of your influence.** You cannot make every student deeply engage with every cognitive operation in every assignment. This was true before AI. What you can do is create conditions where the rational path goes through the cognitive work. Beyond that, students are adults making choices in complex life circumstances. Design for the students you have, not the students you wish you had.


## The Evaluation Paradox as Foundation

Underlying the design principles and patterns is the evaluation paradox: to evaluate whether a tool (or a strategy, or a shortcut) helps you learn, you must already have the knowledge that learning was supposed to produce. A novice cannot judge whether an AI-generated analysis is correct, complete, or relevant, precisely because judging it requires the expertise the assignment was meant to develop. This is not an argument against using AI. It is an argument for designing learning so that the cognitive work cannot be delegated without consequence.


## What This Workshop Is Not

This workshop does not teach participants how to detect AI-generated text. It does not help them write AI usage policies. It does not demonstrate AI tools or discuss prompt engineering. It does not take a position on whether students should or should not use AI.

It teaches instructional design. It uses AI as a diagnostic lens, a way of revealing weaknesses that were always present, and it produces redesigned assessments that are robust not because they are AI-proof but because they are well-designed.
